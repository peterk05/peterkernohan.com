Low-Cost Assistive Robotic Arms (SO-101)

A two-arm robot built from open-source SO-101 arms that learns everyday tasks from demonstration instead of being programmed for each one. Our first imitation-learning policy pours rice live on the arms, and measuring what our demonstrations cover showed us where the data runs out.

Results so far

Measuring what the demonstrations cover

Imitation learning only knows the situations it has been shown. Before training the cube-sorting policy, I wrote scripts that read every recorded episode and map where blocks sat and where each arm grabbed and released. After 43 demonstrations the maps showed whole areas of the table that no demonstration had used, and empty patches inside each arm’s reach. We placed blocks in those areas in the next sessions and grew the dataset to 65 demonstrations.

Where it falls short, and what’s next

  • Every gap costs teleoperation. Filling the coverage holes meant recording more demonstrations by hand, and the demonstrations rarely show how to recover from a missed grasp.
  • Score failures by position. Next I want to test the sorting policy across the coverage grid, so each failure can be traced to the part of the workspace the data missed.
  • Generate the missing experience. I want to try reinforcement learning in simulation to create extra episodes, including recovery from failed grasps, and measure whether adding them improves the policy on the real arms.

Why low-cost assistive arms

People with limited hand or arm function often rely on carers for small, repeated tasks such as pouring a drink or putting things away. Assistive robot arms that can do this already exist, but they typically cost tens of thousands of euros and need an engineer to program every new task.

Our approach is to pair affordable, open-source SO-101 arms with Hugging Face’s LeRobot software, so a new task can be taught by demonstrating it with a leader arm rather than writing code for every movement. Training and inference run on our own laptops, keeping the system cheap to build and easy for others to replicate.

Designed with the community

We contacted nursing homes and Men’s Sheds Ireland, then visited Naas Men’s Shed to see how members work day to day and ask which jobs they find difficult. We showed photos and videos of the arms sorting objects, pouring and picking and placing, and toured the workshop, polytunnels and common rooms.

Two tasks came directly out of that visit:

  • Pouring a drink. Tea is part of every project at the shed, and a mug was dropped and broken while we were there. Pouring hot liquid is hard and risky for anyone with a tremor or arthritis, so one arm holds the cup while the other pours.
  • Tool pick-and-place. Members suggested a robot that returns tools, such as a chisel, to the right spot in their holder. The same skill carries over to sorting, packing and tidying.

Shed members have been invited to Maynooth to try the arms in person, and we plan to return to the shed with the next version to gather hands-on feedback.

Development process

01

Training platform

We designed and built a wooden training board with the follower arms bolted down, so the setup stays rigid and repeatable between data-collection sessions and there is no re-calibration every time the arms are moved.

02

Teleoperation and gripper testing

With the platform built, we teleoperated the follower arms from the leader arms to check range of movement and gripper strength, including picking up round objects with PLA+ printed grippers.

03

Imitation learning

The first model is trained in LeRobot from human demonstrations recorded on the leader arms, with simulation data as a second source. Published work on low-cost bimanual arms using Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT), and compact vision-language-action models such as SmolVLA tested on SO-101 tasks, guides our choice of policies. Our first trained policy now pours rice from a cup into a bowl, running live on the arms.

04

Checking what the data covers

Before training the cube-sorting policy, I mapped where every recorded demonstration placed blocks and where each arm grabbed and released, then used the gaps to plan the next recording sessions (see the coverage maps).

05

Test, gather feedback, retrain

Pouring is tested with rice standing in for liquid and compared against human pours by counting spills; pick-and-place is scored on whether each object ends up in the right place. Feedback from the Men’s Shed and our facilitator feeds a second, improved model, which we compare against the first.

06

Safety and sustainability

A signed risk assessment covers the workshop and moving arms; training uses cold water instead of tea, with electronics kept well clear of liquids. The project is mapped to UN Sustainable Development Goals 11, 12 and 16 through accessible, low-cost, open-source assistive technology.

Competitions

I’m using the same SO-101 and LeRobot platform for the Robot Arm AI Challenge at the IEEE Global Robotics Championship in Malta (28–29 November 2026), where I’m representing IEEE UK & Ireland in a two-person team. The gripper and teleoperation tests on this page, including handling dodecahedron and hexagonal-prism pieces, feed directly into that build. In December I’m competing in the Intel Physical AI Challenge in Zurich, and in September I built a rover-mounted sorting arm at the Alien Bazaar hackathon in Warsaw (see that project).
